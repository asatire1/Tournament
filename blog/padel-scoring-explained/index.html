Padel Scoring Explained: Points, Sets & Tournament Formats

Scoring in padel can be confusing at first. The basic match format borrows heavily from tennis, but once you step into the world of social tournaments, things work quite differently. Whether you are new to the sport or simply trying to make sense of a tournament leaderboard, this guide breaks down every scoring system you are likely to encounter on court.

Regular Padel Match Scoring

A standard padel match uses the same point progression as tennis. Points within a game go 15, 30, 40, game. If both sides reach 40, it is called deuce, and one team must win two consecutive points to take the game — the first of those points is known as advantage.

Games are grouped into sets. The first team to win six games takes the set, provided they lead by at least two games. If the set reaches 6-6, a tiebreak is played. In a tiebreak, points are counted numerically (1, 2, 3...) and the first team to reach 7 points with a two-point margin wins the set.

Most competitive padel matches are played as the best of three sets. This format is great for formal play, but a single match can easily last over an hour, which makes it impractical when you have a group of players and limited court time.

Tournament Scoring is Different

In social and club tournaments, you typically do not play full sets. Instead, matches are played to a fixed number of points — commonly 16, 24, or 32 points per match. Every point counts, and there are no games or sets to track. This keeps rounds short, predictable, and easy to schedule when many players need court time.

The fixed-point system also means that every single rally matters. There is no coasting through a service game or relying on set leads. Matches stay tight from the first point to the last, which is exactly what makes tournament padel so exciting.

Americano Scoring

Americano is the most popular social tournament format. Each player accumulates individual points across all rounds. Your partner changes between rounds, but your points carry over throughout the tournament.

Here is how it works in practice: if you and your partner win a match 16-10, you each earn 16 points to your personal total. Your opponents each receive 10 points. After several rounds with different partners, the player with the highest cumulative total wins the tournament.

Because partners rotate, Americano rewards consistent individual performance rather than relying on one strong pairing. It is a brilliant format for mixing skill levels and making sure everyone has fun.

Mexicano Scoring

Mexicano uses the same individual point accumulation as Americano, but adds a twist: dynamic matchmaking. After each round, players with similar point totals are grouped together for the next round. This means that as the tournament progresses, the top players face each other while those further down the leaderboard also play closely matched opponents.

The result is a tournament that gets tighter and more competitive as it goes on. Instead of a runaway leader building an unassailable advantage, Mexicano naturally creates close races at every level of the leaderboard. It is the format of choice when you want maximum drama heading into the final rounds.

Mix Tournament Scoring

The Mix format is designed for larger groups, typically 20 to 28 players. Players are divided into skill tiers of four players each. Over the course of 15 rounds, both within-tier and cross-tier matches take place, ensuring everyone gets a good variety of opponents and partners.

Points accumulate individually, just like in Americano and Mexicano. The tier system ensures balanced competition: stronger players are tested by each other, while developing players get competitive matches at their own level. It is a format that keeps large events organised without sacrificing fairness.

What About Tie-Breakers?

When two or more players finish a tournament with the same number of points, tie-breakers are used to separate them. The most common approach is to check the head-to-head record between the tied players. If they played each other during the tournament, whoever won that encounter takes the higher position. If head-to-head does not resolve it, point difference (total points scored minus total points conceded) is typically used as a secondary tie-breaker. This ensures that every point you score, even in a losing match, can matter at the end of the tournament.

Once you understand these systems, padel scoring is straightforward. Regular matches follow tennis conventions, while tournament formats strip things back to a simple, point-per-rally model that keeps everything moving. The beauty of Americano, Mexicano, and Mix is that the maths is dead simple — the player with the most points at the end wins — but the strategies and drama that unfold along the way are anything but.
